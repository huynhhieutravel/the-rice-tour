# Cu Chi Tunnels: The Complete Travel Guide

The Cu Chi Tunnels are often misunderstood before people set foot here. Most travelers expect an intense, claustrophobic underground trek. What you actually encounter northwest of Ho Chi Minh City is an open-air historical site set under regenerated forest canopy—the surviving core of a wartime stronghold hand-chiseled out of dense red clay that once sheltered fighters, field medics, and entire village families beneath sustained bombardment.

Today, nobody wanders through 250 kilometers of tunnels. Instead, two separate visitor areas—**Ben Dinh** and **Ben Duoc**—offer preserved and adapted sections where you can see camouflaged trapdoors, examine disguised air vents, inspect reconstructed booby traps, crawl short tunnel segments, and understand how an entire community ate, slept, and worked underground for years.

Most of your time is spent outdoors along shaded dirt trails. You'll walk past bomb craters, watch archival wartime briefings in open-air thatch pavilions, examine captured weaponry, and taste the steamed cassava that kept resistance fighters alive.

Your main planning decision isn't whether to go—it's deciding which sector suits your pace, figuring out how early you're willing to wake up, and choosing how you want to handle the drive.

## What Are The Cu Chi Tunnels?

The Cu Chi Tunnels aren't a single cavern complex. They are the surviving remnants of an underground fortification network built across the rural district of Cu Chi, roughly 50 to 70 kilometers northwest of downtown Ho Chi Minh City.

The network didn't start as a massive 250-kilometer underground city. Following the outbreak of the French resistance in southern Vietnam, local villagers and militia began digging simple individual hiding pits (*hầm bí mật*) starting around 1946. Around 1948, these isolated shelters in Tan Phu Trung and Phuoc Vinh An communes were interconnected into the first continuous subterranean passages of about 17 km. Between 1961 and 1965, facing escalating aerial bombardment and ground sweeps across the "Iron Triangle," local forces expanded the network into a fortified, three-tiered defensive labyrinth linking entire communes.

Understanding that evolution matters for visitors today.

When you arrive at Cu Chi, you are not stepping into an endless underground maze. Most of the original wartime network collapsed or was reclaimed by farmland and jungle after 1975. Today, roughly 4 km of passages are conserved and maintained across the two main visitor sectors at Ben Duoc and Ben Dinh, with additional historical traces surviving at Xom Bung, An Binh, and Cay Da.

The preserved visitor sites show how subterranean spaces fulfilled everyday survival functions:
- **Concealed Movement:** Shifting troops and supplies beneath artillery barrages without being spotted from above.
- **Ambush Points:** Camouflaged trapdoors letting fighters strike quickly and vanish back into the earth.
- **Living Quarters:** Sleeping platforms with hammock hooks, storage recesses, and fresh air ducts.
- **Medical Bunkers:** Operating rooms lit by modified kerosene lamps where doctors performed surgery on wounded soldiers.
- **Ordnance Workshops:** Reclaiming unexploded American bombs and shells to forge punji traps, mines, and grenades.
- **Command Posts:** Meeting chambers for regional military commanders and district leadership committees.

### Life Underground Was More Than Warfare

Life below ground wasn't just firefights and combat. For weeks at a time, soldiers, medics, and local civilians lived entirely beneath the surface.

UNESCO's 2026 nomination dossier highlights spaces dedicated to resting, community meetings, grain storage, hand-dug wells, medical care, and even makeshift theatrical performances. Children attended basic lessons under the glow of oil lamps, cobblers cut tire sandals from downed aircraft wreckage, and cultural troupes sang folk songs to maintain morale during weeks of intense bombing. That is why Vietnamese historians refer to Cu Chi as an underground combat village (*làng hầm*) rather than simply a military trench.

## A Brief History & Multi-Tier Architecture

Digging through Cu Chi's soil was grueling manual labor. Guerrillas worked mostly at night using short-handled triangular hoes (*cuốc*) and woven bamboo baskets, dumping excavated dirt into fresh bomb craters or into the Saigon River to avoid detection from reconnaissance planes.

The local soil—an iron-rich, reddish-brown laterite clay—turned out to be an ideal engineering material. When freshly dug, it is pliable enough to carve by hand. But once exposed to air and dried, laterite cures to near-brick density. Carved with vaulted, curved ceilings, these tunnels withstood heavy aerial bombardment and the weight of passing tanks without collapsing.

Between 1965 and 1968, the network evolved into three distinct depth tiers:
1. **First Tier (approx. 3 meters deep):** Built with firing slits, communication trenches, and zigzag corners designed to break the shockwaves of exploding artillery shells.
2. **Second Tier (approx. 5 to 6 meters deep):** Housed living spaces, kitchens, supply depots, meeting rooms, and field hospitals.
3. **Third Tier (approx. 8 to 10+ meters deep):** The deepest sanctuary, containing emergency command shelters, fresh water wells, and secret escape tunnels running toward the Saigon River. At Ben Duoc, an original 20-meter tunnel section still leads toward the riverbank today.

## How The Underground Network Functioned

![Licensed tour guide explaining the three-tiered underground network using a detailed cross-section model diorama at Cu Chi](https://media.thericetour.com/uploads/guide-explaining-cu-chi-tunnel-cross-section-model-diorama.webp)

### 1. Concealed Entrances (*Cửa Hầm Bí Mật*)
Surface entry shafts were cut just wide enough for a slender human frame—about 25 by 40 cm. Wooden trapdoor lids were plastered with clay and scattered with dry forest leaves. On-site guides still demonstrate how a person could drop feet-first into the shaft, pull the lid flush with the forest floor, and vanish in under three seconds.

![A demonstrator holds open the wooden leaf-covered trapdoor hatch, illustrating how it seals flush with the earthen forest floor](https://media.thericetour.com/uploads/man-holding-leaf-covered-tunnel-cover-secret-entrance-cu-chi.webp)

### 2. Disguised Air Vents
People living underground needed fresh air without giving away their position. Air shafts were drilled sideways through clay seams, venting out through hollow bamboo tubes hidden inside natural termite mounds (*ụ mối*) or rotten tree stumps. The holes were angled downward so monsoon downpours couldn't flood the chambers below.

### 3. The Smokeless Hoàng Cầm Stove (*Bếp Hoàng Cầm*)
Cooking underground was dangerous because rising smoke drew immediate airstrikes. In late 1951, military cook Hoàng Cầm invented a stove with underground exhaust flues. Smoke was channeled through long, gently sloping trenches covered by branches and tamped earth. By the time it traveled 20 meters to the surface, the smoke had cooled and dispersed along the ground like early morning forest mist.

### 4. Fresh Water & River Exits
Hand-dug wells reached down to clean groundwater veins on the third tier. Hidden underwater exits also opened below the waterline of the Saigon River, letting couriers and scouts swim out into river currents unseen by riverbank patrols.

## Are The Cu Chi Tunnels You Crawl Through Original?

> 🔍 **Authenticity & Conservation Reality**  
> The short answer: Yes, they are authentic wartime tunnels, but they have been significantly adapted for tourism. You are not entering an untouched 1968 combat trench. Following heavy wartime damage, restoration began in 1978 and expanded throughout the 1980s and 1990s. The sections open to the public today have been reinforced with concrete collars, equipped with dim electric lights, and widened by 20% to 30% so international visitors can fit through safely.

## Ben Dinh vs. Ben Duoc: The Definitive Sector Choice

When booking a trip from Saigon, many travelers don't realize there are two separate visitor sites operated by the relic management board: **Ben Dinh** and **Ben Duoc**.

![Travelers checking in at the forested entrance plaza of Cu Chi Tunnels before beginning the historical circuit](https://media.thericetour.com/uploads/tourists-check-in-cu-chi-ben-dinh-historic-site-the-rice.webp)

![Demonstration of a camouflaged leaf trapdoor entrance at the Cu Chi forested visitor sector](https://media.thericetour.com/uploads/group-posing-secret-tunnel-entrance-leaves-cu-chi-1280x720.webp)

Choosing between them is the single biggest factor shaping your visit:

### Ben Dinh Sector (Nhuan Duc Commune)
- **Location:** Roughly 50 km northwest of District 1 (about 1.5 to 2 hours drive).
- **Wartime Role:** Headquarters of the Cu Chi District Party Committee.
- **Atmosphere:** Ben Dinh handles the vast majority of commercial day-tour buses because it's closer to the city with wide access roads and large parking bays. It has large orientation pavilions, wide paved walkways, and busy souvenir stalls.
- **The Tunnels:** Passages here are heavily widened, fitted with concrete safety collars, and lit throughout.
- **Best For:** Travelers on tight schedules, families wanting shorter driving times, and standard half-day morning tours.

### Ben Duoc Sector (Phu My Hung Commune)
- **Location:** Roughly 65 to 70 km northwest of District 1 (about 2 to 2.5 hours drive).
- **Wartime Role:** Command headquarters for the Saigon–Gia Dinh Military Zone and Regional Party Committee (*Khu ủy Sài Gòn - Chợ Lớn - Gia Định*).
- **Atmosphere:** Set among peaceful rubber tree plantations and native forest along the Saigon River, Ben Duoc sees far fewer large tour coaches. The atmosphere is quiet, unhurried, and noticeably more reflective.
- **Key Landmark:** The monumental **Ben Duoc Martyrs Memorial Temple** (*Đền Bến Dược*), a 7-hectare commemorative sanctuary honoring 44,752 fallen heroes, martyrs, and Vietnamese Heroic Mothers inscribed on stone steles.
- **The Tunnels:** Offers a more authentic feeling, including multiple depth levels alongside reinforced visitor paths.
- **Best For:** History enthusiasts, photographers, independent travelers, and anyone exploring by private car who prefers quiet woodland over tourist crowds.

### Side-by-Side Comparison: Ben Dinh vs. Ben Duoc

| Feature | Ben Dinh Sector | Ben Duoc Sector |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Commune Location** | Nhuan Duc Commune | Phu My Hung Commune |

| **Typical Visitor Flow** | High bus volume (commercial tour hub) | Generally uncrowded (independent & local visitors) |
| **Historical Role** | Cu Chi District Party Committee | Saigon–Gia Dinh Regional Zone Command |
| **Memorial Landmark** | None on-site | Ben Duoc Martyrs Memorial Temple (7 ha) |
| **Tunnel Experience** | Conserved and adapted visitor sections | Conserved and adapted visitor sections |
| **Best Suited To** | Shorter logistics & compact half-day schedules | Travelers willing to spend longer on the road for quiet forest setting |

## What Happens During A Typical Visit?

![Tour guide demonstrating hidden trapdoors and wooden punji stick traps in the forest of Cu Chi](https://media.thericetour.com/uploads/guide-showing-punji-stick-traps-cu-chi-tunnels-tour.webp)

Most visits take 2 to 2.5 hours on-site, following a structured loop through forest paths and underground demonstration points:

1. **Documentary & Relief Map Briefing:** You start in an open-air thatched pavilion, watching black-and-white archival film from the 1960s and studying three-dimensional relief models of the multi-tiered defense system.
2. **Forest Walk & Camouflage Displays:** Your guide leads you along dirt trails beneath rubber and mahogany trees, pointing out bomb craters, defoliation scars, and leaf-covered entrance shafts.
3. **Trapdoor Entry Demonstration:** On-site guides demonstrate sliding feet-first into micro-shafts. Anyone comfortable can step inside the trapdoor for a classic photo.
4. **Defensive Trap Gallery:** Reconstructed punji stick traps, swinging spike arms, rolling traps, and folding-door traps crafted from sharpened bamboo and salvaged metal.
5. **Wartime Artifacts & Tanks:** Walk past an authentic American M41 Walker Bulldog tank disabled by a guerrilla mine in 1970, unexploded bomb casings, and a workshop showing how fighters forged tire sandals (*dép lốp*) from aircraft tires.

![Travelers examining an authentic decommissioned American M41 Walker Bulldog tank preserved in the forest canopy at Cu Chi](https://media.thericetour.com/uploads/two-girls-posing-rusted-tank-cu-chi-tunnels-the-rice.webp)
6. **The Optional Tunnel Crawl:** An opportunity to experience the subterranean passages firsthand. Exits are spaced every 15 to 20 meters so you can climb out whenever you wish.
7. **Smokeless Kitchen & Cassava Tasting:** Stop at a reconstructed Hoàng Cầm kitchen to see the underground smoke-dispersion vents, then sit down for hot boiled cassava dipped in crushed roasted peanuts, sugar, and salt (*khoai mì chấm muối mè*) with hot tea.
8. **Optional Shooting Range:** On the outer perimeter, the National Defense Sports Shooting Range allows visitors over 18 to fire historic military rifles under military supervision. **Good to know:** Gunfire from the range echoes through the woods and is clearly audible along the walking trails. If you're expecting dead quiet, the sudden crack of rifle fire can catch you off guard.

## Do You Have To Crawl Through The Tunnels?

**No. Entering the subterranean tunnels is 100% voluntary.**

A common worry among first-time visitors is that visiting Cu Chi requires crawling on your knees through pitch-black dirt. That isn't how the tour works.

In reality, **over 90% of the experience takes place above ground** along shaded forest trails:
- Reconstructed bunkers, surgical triage rooms, and command headquarters have open roofs or wide stairwells with safety handrails.
- Trapdoor demonstrations, booby-trap exhibitions, and diorama models are all viewed from ground level.
- You can watch the documentary, inspect the tank, examine the bomb craters, and eat the cassava without ever ducking underground.

If anyone in your group has bad knees, back issues, heart conditions, or claustrophobia, they can relax comfortably under shaded pavilions while others try the crawl.

## What Does Crawling Actually Feel Like?

If you do choose to enter the tunnel, knowing what to expect physically helps avoid panic:

- **Dimensions:** Even in widened tourist sections (~80 cm wide by 120 cm high), adults cannot stand upright. You must crouch, duck-waddle, or crawl on hands and knees.
- **The atmosphere:** It's warm (typically 32°C to 36°C) and humid. The air smells intensely of dry soil and clay. Low-voltage bulbs provide dim yellow light every few meters.
- **The physical effort:** Duck-walking burns your thighs within thirty seconds. A 20-meter crawl takes only two minutes, but you will emerge sweating and breathing hard.
- **Emergency exits:** Passages have exit ladders every 15 to 20 meters. You don't have to commit to a long crawl—try the first 15 meters, and if your knees ache or the close space bothers you, climb right out.

### Is The Tunnel Suitable For Tall Or Larger Travelers?

Tunnel sections open to visitors have been enlarged compared with original wartime passages, but they can still feel remarkably tight for tall or broader travelers. If you are over 6 ft (183 cm), have broad shoulders, or dislike crouching in confined spaces, test the first 15-meter stretch before deciding whether to continue.

> 💡 **Practical Rule:** If you suffer from acute claustrophobia, asthma, high blood pressure, or joint pain, step into the entrance shaft for a photo, then climb straight back out. Nobody will judge you for skipping the crawl.

## Why Traveler Experiences At Cu Chi Differ So Much

Visitor reviews of Cu Chi range from "one of the most moving historical sites in Southeast Asia" to "crowded, rushed, and commercialized." That difference almost always comes down to three things:

1. **Group Size & Arrival Timing:** Arriving at Ben Dinh at 10:15 AM puts you right behind twenty 45-seat tour coaches. You'll wait in line for the trapdoor photo op, squeeze into crowded briefing pavilions, and move at a herd pace. Leaving Saigon at 07:30 AM in a private car lets you explore empty trails in the cool morning air.
2. **The Guide's Perspective:** A dirt crater or a rusty bomb casing is just metal and earth without context. A knowledgeable guide shares personal family history, explains how civilian families survived without sunlight, and answers candid questions about the war.
3. **Pacing Realities:** A thorough visit takes **2 to 2.5 hours on-site**. Trying to stretch Cu Chi into a whole-day tour without combining it with Saigon landmarks just leaves you sitting around in highway traffic.

## Can You Visit Cu Chi Without Booking A Tour?

**Yes. Cu Chi can be visited completely on your own.**

You can hire a private driver, call a Grab taxi, or take public buses from Saigon, buy your ticket at the entrance booth, and join an on-site guide. The 135,000 VND foreign entrance fee includes a site guide stationed at the gate who walks your group through the outdoor circuit and explains the traps.

The trade-off is **convenience versus control**:
- An independent visit gives you full freedom over your timetable and pacing, but you handle highway navigation, parking, and timing on your own.
- A private guide and driver handles door-to-door pickup, times your arrival to avoid bus surges, and gives you continuous, personalized storytelling from start to finish.

## Weather And Season

Cu Chi can be visited year-round, but seasonal weather affects comfort along the forest trails:

- **Dry Season (December to April):** Generally drier, less humid, and more comfortable for walking under the forest canopy. Ground trails are firm and easy to navigate. Morning departures remain ideal to avoid midday heat (32°C–35°C+).
- **Rainy Season (May to November):** Brings frequent afternoon downpours and higher humidity. Forest dirt trails can become muddy and slick. If visiting during these months, opt for morning tours to complete your walk before afternoon showers arrive.

## What to Wear & Practical Packing List

Cu Chi is an outdoor forested archaeological site characterized by unpaved dirt trails, laterite dust, and tropical humidity.

### Recommended Attire
- **Loose, Breathable Fabrics:** Linen, lightweight cotton, or moisture-wicking athletic wear.
- **Dark or Earth-Tone Colors:** Red laterite clay leaves permanent reddish-brown stains on white clothing and pristine shoes if you crawl through the tunnels or brush against trench walls. Many travelers wrap a traditional cotton [khăn rằn checkered scarf](/khan-ran-nam-bo) around their neck to absorb perspiration and protect against the sun.
- **Sturdy Closed-Toe Shoes:** Sneakers or walking shoes with good traction. Avoid flip-flops, slides, or high heels.
- **Temple-Appropriate Clothing (Ben Duoc only):** If visiting the Ben Duoc Memorial Temple, shoulders and knees must be respectfully covered.

### Essential Packing Checklist
- 🧴 **High-SPF Sunscreen & Insect Repellent:** Dense woodland canopy harbors mosquitoes.
- 🧢 **Wide-Brim Hat or Cap:** Essential for sunny clearing sections.
- 💧 **Reusable Water Bottle:** Staying hydrated in tropical humidity is vital.
- 🧻 **Wet Wipes or Hand Sanitizer:** Indispensable for wiping red clay off your hands and knees after tunnel crawling.
- 💵 **Cash in Vietnamese Dong (VND):** Entrance tickets, drinks, and firing range bullets require cash on-site. Mobile data and card terminals can be spotty under the trees.

## Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

### How far are the Cu Chi Tunnels from downtown Ho Chi Minh City?
Cu Chi is roughly 50 to 70 kilometers northwest of central Ho Chi Minh City by road, depending on the visitor sector and route. Ben Dinh is closer (~50 km; 1.5 to 2 hours), while Ben Duoc lies farther (~70 km; 2 to 2.5 hours). Travel time matters more than headline distance because traffic along Highway 22 can significantly lengthen the journey.

### Which site should you visit: Ben Dinh or Ben Duoc?
Neither site should be simplified as "authentic" versus "commercial"—both have been conserved and adapted for visitor safety. Ben Dinh (Nhuan Duc Commune) is closer and best suited for travelers on compact half-day schedules. Ben Duoc (Phu My Hung Commune) is farther, significantly quieter, and features the monumental Ben Duoc Memorial Temple alongside multi-level tunnel networks.

### Are the Cu Chi Tunnels you crawl through original?
They are authentic historic tunnel areas, but the visitor experience is not the same as entering an untouched wartime tunnel. Sections open to tourists have been conserved, repaired, reinforced, and in places enlarged or equipped with electric lighting for safety and accessibility. The original network was far larger than the sections visitors can explore today.

### Why does a 250 km tunnel network have only about 4 km preserved today?
The 250 km figure represents the estimated cumulative footprint of the wartime subterranean system at its historical peak. Over decades of conflict and post-war agricultural expansion, large portions collapsed or were reclaimed by nature. Today, the two principal conserved areas at Ben Duoc (~8.4 ha) and Ben Dinh (~6.71 ha) preserve approximately 4 km of tunnel remains for research, education, and heritage access.

### Is Cu Chi suitable for tall or larger travelers?
Visitor tunnels have been enlarged compared with original wartime passages, but they can still feel extremely tight. If you are over 6 ft (183 cm), have broad shoulders, or dislike crouching in confined spaces, you can test the first short 15–20 meter section before deciding whether to continue, or choose to enjoy the above-ground exhibits entirely.

### Can you visit Cu Chi independently without booking a tour?
Yes. Cu Chi can be visited independently. Travelers can arrange their own transport (via private car, taxi/Grab, or public bus), purchase admission at the gate, and utilize the on-site guiding service included in the 135,000 VND foreign visitor tariff. A private tour adds convenience with door-to-door transport, tailored departure timing, and deeper personalized interpretation.

### Are Cu Chi and Vinh Moc Tunnels the same?
No. They served very different historical and geographic purposes. Cu Chi (near Saigon) was an active tactical military fortification and guerrilla combat base carved through red clay, featuring very tight passages. Vinh Moc (in Quang Tri Province along the DMZ) was an underground coastal village excavated through basalt rock where hundreds of civilians lived for months to shelter from heavy aerial bombardment.

### Are there other wartime tunnels in Ho Chi Minh City?
Yes. Other tunnel heritage sites exist in the region, such as the Phu Tho Hoa tunnel relic in Tan Phu District, which dates back to the French colonial resistance. However, Cu Chi remains by far the largest, most intact, and most comprehensive wartime tunnel network open to visitors in southern Vietnam.

### Do you have to crawl through a tunnel?
No. Entering the tunnels is 100% voluntary. Over 90% of the exhibits, demonstrations, bunkers, and relics are situated above ground along shaded, accessible trails.

### How much does Cu Chi Tunnels admission cost in 2026?
Official foreign-visitor admission is 135,000 VND per person (comprising 35,000 VND gate admission and 100,000 VND heritage and site-guide fee). Vietnamese citizens pay 35,000 VND. Shooting range ammunition is sold separately.

### What is the UNESCO status of Cu Chi Tunnels in 2026?
Cu Chi Tunnels was added to Vietnam's UNESCO World Heritage Tentative List on July 15, 2026, under cultural criteria *(iv)* and *(vi)*. It is on the nomination roster, not yet an inscribed UNESCO World Heritage Site.

### Can claustrophobic travelers enjoy Cu Chi?
Yes. Claustrophobic visitors can thoroughly enjoy the open-air walking tour, examine trap mechanisms, watch the introductory documentary, inspect above-ground bunkers, and skip the underground crawl entirely without feeling left out.

### Can you combine Cu Chi Tunnels and the Mekong Delta in one day?
While commercially available through budget agencies, we strongly advise against it. The combined itinerary requires 6.5 to 7.5 hours inside a vehicle covering over 200 kilometers of congested highway, leaving you exhausted with minimal time at each destination. Dedicate a half-day to Cu Chi and explore the Mekong Delta on a separate day.

## Visiting Cu Chi: Final Practical Thoughts

Cu Chi is rarely what travelers picture from guidebooks beforehand. It isn't an amusement park ride, nor is it an endless underground maze where you spend hours crawling on your hands and knees.

Most of your time is spent walking quietly through regenerated woodland, looking at simple tools, leaf-covered trapdoors, and cleverly channeled smoke flues that kept thousands of people alive beneath intense aerial bombardment.

If you depart early to beat the bus convoys, wear clothes you don't mind getting dusty, and take time to absorb the human realities rather than rushing between photo ops, Cu Chi offers one of the most sobering and educational day trips you can take in [Southern Vietnam](/destination/south-vietnam).
